Penobscot: Know Before You Vote

November 20 Special Election: Authorizing Bars & Restaurants in Penobscot

Penobscot’s Land Use Regulations (adopted 1974) require a Planning Board permit for commercial structures in the General Development District. They do not set standards for noise, hours of operation, events or exterior lighting. Penobscot has no site plan review or special amusement ordinance. Several peer towns have rules of this kind. This document examines how Maine’s liquor-licensing statutes interact with local ordinances in Penobscot and neighboring towns, and highlights questions voters and officials may wish to clarify with the town’s attorney before the election.

Summary of Tools Available to Penobscot & Where They Stand

From upfront land-use permitting to standalone nuisance rules and annual liquor license renewals, Penobscot has several distinct legal mechanisms—some already in effect under local or state law, and others that do not exist today without Town Meeting action:

  • 1. 1974 Land Use Regulations — Planning Board Permit (In Effect Today • Proactive, Limited Scope): Requires a Planning Board permit in the General Development District (LUR §§ 5, 6.L, 7.5, 12-B), a soils report, 20-ft side/rear setbacks, and environmental/shoreland findings, with authority to attach reasonable conditions. Contains no standards for noise, operating hours, outdoor events, or exterior lighting.
  • 2. Commercial Site Plan Review Ordinance & Optional Moratorium (Not in Place • Proactive; Requires Town Meeting Votes): Called for in Penobscot’s Comprehensive Plan to establish upfront site design rules (buffers, lighting, parking, traffic, noise) for all new commercial development. Both a temporary moratorium (30-A M.R.S. § 4356) and a new ordinance require Town Meeting approval; under zoning law, already-vested businesses can claim grandfathered status against later land-use changes.
  • 3. Standalone Special Amusement & Commercial Noise/Nuisance Ordinance (Not in Place • Proactive & Ongoing; Requires Town Meeting Vote): Can be enacted at a Special Town Meeting under 28-A M.R.S. § 1054 and Home Rule police power (30-A M.R.S. § 3001) to require annual Select Board entertainment permits, abutter notice, indoor/outdoor curfews, and property-line noise limits. Because it regulates ongoing conduct and annual licensing rather than zoning, land-use grandfathering does not block it from applying to active businesses upon passage.
  • 4. Initial Liquor License Review by the Select Board (Triggered by “Yes” Vote • Limited to Ordinances in Force): Under 28-A M.R.S. § 1051(2) and § 653, the Select Board holds a public hearing and has 60 days to approve or deny a liquor application. At initial licensing, local-rule grounds for denial (land use § 653(2)(B), entertainment permits § 653(2)(D-1), server training § 653(2)(G)) can only be used if Penobscot has actually enacted those local rules.
  • 5. Annual License Renewal Input & “Conditions of Record” Backstop (In Effect Statewide • Reactive Backstop at Renewal): Under 28-A M.R.S. § 653(2)(C), no local ordinance is required for the Select Board—after public hearing and neighbor input at annual renewal—to deny a license based on documented waste, health/safety, repeated parking/traffic violations, or patron/employee conduct unreasonably interfering with neighbors’ reasonable use of their property. Reactive only: protects neighbors once a record of violations exists, but cannot be used to deny a new proposal on anticipated concerns alone.

How the Penobscot Vote, State Licensing & Local Rules Fit Together

REF: MAINE BABLO GUIDANCE & 28-A M.R.S. §§ 1051, 653, 1054 • § 653(2)(C) CONDITIONS OF RECORD • POST-HEARING UPDATE

What a yes vote in Penobscot does and doesn’t do. A yes vote on November 20 would let the State issue licenses for on-premises liquor sales in Penobscot. It would not approve any specific business or limit how many licenses can be issued in Penobscot. The Maine Bureau of Alcoholic Beverages and Lottery Operations (BABLO) confirmed that the State has no population-based limit on the number of on-premises licenses in a municipality.

What happens next. An applicant in Penobscot applies to BABLO, but the initial application—and each annual renewal—must be approved by the Penobscot Select Board (28-A M.R.S. §1051(2), §653). The Select Board must give written reasons and may deny only on grounds listed in §653(2). Several grounds depend on local rules: noncompliance with a local land use ordinance (§653(2)(B)), failure to comply with a municipal entertainment permit (§653(2)(D-1)), and, where required locally, server training (§653(2)(G)). Another ground—documented “conditions of record” (§653(2)(C))—applies statewide without a local ordinance once a business has an operating track record. If the Select Board takes no final action within 60 days of filing, the application is deemed approved (§653(1)(C)). A denial can be appealed to BABLO within 15 days.

Why Penobscot’s ordinances matter. Generally, when reviewing an initial application, the Penobscot Select Board can apply only the local ordinances in force at that time. Where a town has no standards for noise, local operating hours, events or lighting, those matters are not listed local-ordinance grounds for denial.

Reader-Submitted Update • Ongoing Community Input Encouraged

A State-Law Backstop for Neighbors: “Conditions of Record” (28-A M.R.S. § 653(2)(C))

A reader alerted us to a provision of Maine’s liquor-licensing law that provides a meaningful safeguard for neighbors even in towns with minimal local ordinances. Under 28-A M.R.S. § 653(2)(C), the Penobscot Select Board has statutory authority to deny a liquor license—most notably when an operating business applies for annual renewal—based on “conditions of record” such as waste disposal violations, health or safety violations, repeated parking or traffic violations near the premises, or other conditions caused by patrons or employees that unreasonably disturb or interfere with neighbors’ reasonable use and enjoyment of their property.

  • A real tool to protect neighbors’ enjoyment of their property: Even without a local site plan or noise ordinance on Penobscot’s books, state law does not leave neighbors without recourse. Because liquor licenses must be renewed annually with the Select Board, § 653(2)(C) gives the town a built-in mechanism to hold an operator accountable if a venue’s patrons, parking, waste, or operations unreasonably interfere with neighboring homes or businesses.
  • Applies in every Maine town without a local ordinance: Unlike other grounds in § 653(2)—which depend on whether a town has adopted local land-use rules (B), entertainment permits (D-1), or server-training requirements (G)—§ 653(2)(C) is written directly into state law and is available to Penobscot’s Select Board today.
  • Reactive enforcement vs. proactive standards: Local site plan and special amusement ordinances are proactive—they set clear expectations in advance for buffers, lighting, parking layout, decibel limits, and closing hours so conflicts are prevented before a venue is built or opens. By contrast, § 653(2)(C) is reactive: it can only be used after problems have already occurred and been documented, and it cannot be used to deny a new business’s initial application based on anticipated concerns alone.
  • Depends on a documented “record” & written reasons: The statute requires “conditions of record,” though it does not define what documentation meets that threshold, and we are not aware of state guidance on that point. Any denial requires written reasons from the Select Board and can be appealed by the applicant to BABLO within 15 days (§ 653(3)).

We welcome additional statutory references and factual corrections from residents and officials as we continue updating this resource.

Post-Public Hearing Update • Incorporating Citizen Input

Can Penobscot Adopt Rules After a Business Opens? Land Use Grandfathering vs. Standalone Police-Power Ordinances

At the Penobscot public hearing, citizens—including someone with restaurant-owning experience in another state—pointed out that municipalities can adopt operational rules that apply to existing businesses even after they open. Maine statutes make an important distinction in how Penobscot would enact those rules:

  • Why a Land Use Amendment Is Vulnerable to Grandfathering: If noise, hours, or entertainment controls are placed only inside Penobscot’s Land Use Regulations, existing or pending commercial operations can often claim vested rights or grandfathered (“nonconforming”) status under zoning law.
  • How a Standalone Police-Power Ordinance Applies Immediately: Instead of amending the Land Use Regulations, Penobscot’s legislative body (Town Meeting) can enact a standalone Special Amusement & Public Nuisance/Noise Ordinance under Maine’s Special Amusement statute (28-A M.R.S. §1054) and municipal home-rule police power (30-A M.R.S. §3001). Because police-power nuisance rules and annual Special Amusement permits regulate ongoing conduct and annual licensing rather than land-use classification, grandfathering does not insulate an active establishment from complying upon passage.
  • Select Board Agility & Non-Zoning Focus: Under §1054, the Penobscot Select Board serves directly as the licensing authority—empowered to hold public hearings with written notice to abutters (e.g., within 300–500 feet), set fixed outdoor/indoor curfews (such as 9:00 PM outdoors and 10:30 or 11:00 PM indoors, as in Sedgwick and Blue Hill), enforce objective property-line noise standards (a “plainly audible” rule or decibel caps, as in Castine), attach site-specific conditions, and suspend or revoke permits for violations without tying up the Planning Board or triggering broader zoning debates.

Such a standalone ordinance can be voted on at a Special Town Meeting as an immediate safeguard and include a cross-reference clause bridging to any future Commercial Site Plan Review ordinance.

Source: Email from Sara Morey, Licensing and Tax Supervisor, BABLO Liquor Licensing and Enforcement (Oct. 23, 2026); Maine Revised Statutes Title 28-A (§§ 1051, 653, 1054) and Title 30-A (§ 3001). Voters and officials should confirm specific ordinance strategies with Penobscot’s town attorney or the Maine Municipal Association.

1. Penobscot: Planning Board Permit, No Commercial Performance Standards

REF: PENOBSCOT LAND USE REGULATION SECTIONS 5, 6.L, 12-B

In Penobscot, commercial structures require a Planning Board permit in the General Development District and are prohibited in the other two districts (Section 5). To grant a permit, the Penobscot Planning Board must find that the use will not create unsafe or unhealthful conditions, erosion, water pollution or wildlife habitat damage. It must also find that the use will conserve shoreland vegetation, public access to water and natural beauty (12-B(6)). The Board may attach reasonable conditions (12-B(7)), and commercial development in Penobscot requires a soils report (6.L). None of these Penobscot provisions sets standards for noise, hours of operation, events or exterior lighting.

2. Penobscot: Setbacks

REF: PENOBSCOT LAND USE REGULATION SECTIONS 7.5, 7.10, 7.11

All structures in any Penobscot district must be set back 20 feet from side and rear lot lines (7.5). Penobscot’s regulations as posted state no highway setback for commercial structures, and they do not require vegetative buffers or screening between commercial and residential properties. Commercial establishments in Penobscot, other than professional offices, are not allowed in the Shoreland District or in residential subdivisions (7.10, 7.11).

3. Penobscot: No Mechanism for Noise, Hours, or Lighting Rules

Penobscot’s posted ordinances contain no provision, and no review or permit process, that specifically addresses noise, hours of operation, events or exterior lighting for commercial venues. Each neighboring peer town below has at least one such mechanism, but they differ:

  • Some set numeric limits (Castine on noise, Blue Hill and Brooklin on lighting).
  • Some use general standards applied case by case (Orland, Surry, Brooksville).
  • Sedgwick’s closing times apply only to venues with a special amusement permit.

Penobscot: What’s on (and Potentially on) the Table — Four Related but Legally Distinct Tracks

Community discussion in Penobscot around the November 20 election involves four different tracks. While connected in the public conversation, they are legally separate actions—each governed by different Maine statutes, decided by different bodies, and requiring separate procedural steps:

Track 1 — The November 20 Ballot Vote on On-Premises Alcohol (28-A M.R.S. §§ 123, 1051, 653): A “Yes” vote authorizes the State to issue licenses for bars and alcohol-serving restaurants in Penobscot, allowing any qualified applicant who obtains a license to open in the General Development District so long as they follow Penobscot’s existing 1974 Land Use Regulations (which set minimal commercial standards and contain no noise, hours, events, or lighting rules). This ballot question is not about any specific proposal, even though it is naturally driven by a prospective applicant who cannot proceed or plan an investment without the baseline local-option law changing first.

Track 2 — Eventual Application to the Penobscot Planning Board for a Commercial Permit (LUR §§ 5, 6.L, 12-B) & Select Board Review (§ 653): Any specific bar or restaurant proposal must still apply to the Penobscot Planning Board for a commercial structure permit and meet state and local requirements applicable to commercial development generally, followed by Select Board liquor license review. When reviewing an initial application, both boards can apply only the ordinances in force at that time; once a business is operating, state law (28-A M.R.S. § 653(2)(C)) also gives the Select Board a reactive tool at annual renewal to deny a license based on documented “conditions of record” that unreasonably interfere with neighbors’ reasonable use of their property.

Track 3 — Potential Commercial Development Moratorium (30-A M.R.S. § 4356) to Adopt a Commercial Site Plan Review Ordinance: Residents have expressed interest in a temporary commercial development moratorium until Penobscot modernizes its ordinances to include a Commercial Site Plan Review Ordinance, as called for in Penobscot’s Comprehensive Plan. This would apply to any new commercial establishment town-wide—not just bars or restaurants—and requires separate Town Meeting approval supported by statutory findings of necessity under 30-A M.R.S. § 4356.

Track 4 — Potential Standalone Special Amusement & Commercial Noise Control Ordinance Under Home Rule (28-A M.R.S. § 1054 & 30-A M.R.S. § 3001): Penobscot can independently draft and pass a standalone Special Amusement and Commercial Noise Control Ordinance at a Town Meeting to impose entertainment curfews, neighbor notice, noise limits, and operating conditions on bars and restaurants to protect neighbors.

Post-Public Hearing Update: Architecture of an Interim Special Amusement & Operational Noise Ordinance (28-A M.R.S. § 1054 & 30-A M.R.S. § 3001)

1. Mandatory Special Amusement Permit (28-A M.R.S. § 1054): Any on-premises liquor licensee in Penobscot offering entertainment, live vocal/instrumental music, DJs, or amplified sound (excluding incidental background radio/TV at conversational levels) must obtain an annual permit from the Select Board, with first-class mail notice to property owners within 300–500 feet and a public hearing prior to issuance or renewal.
2. Fixed Curfews for Amplified & Outdoor Activity: Modeled on Sedgwick and Blue Hill practices—e.g., outdoor music, entertainment, and amplified speakers cease at 9:00 PM; indoor entertainment ceases at 10:30 PM or 11:00 PM; exterior patio hours conditioned independently of kitchen hours.
3. Objective Noise & Emitted Sound Standards: A "plainly audible" rule prohibiting sound from instruments, amplifiers, or patron gatherings plainly audible within an abutting residential dwelling or beyond 100 feet of the property line between 9:00 PM and 7:00 AM, optionally paired with decibel limits (e.g., 60 dBA daytime / 50 dBA night, mirroring Castine and Maine DEP Chapter 375 guidelines).
4. Discretionary Conditioning & Site Plan Bridge: Authorizes the Select Board to attach binding written conditions (speaker/seating orientation away from residential abutters, prohibiting outdoor amplified sound on constrained parcels, parking management plans preventing overflow onto public roads, and suspension/revocation triggers after two or more validated complaints within a license year) plus a cross-reference clause making compliance with future Commercial Site Plan Review standards a condition of renewal.

Neighboring Town Ordinance Summaries & Excerpts

Blue Hill

Official Website: bluehillme.gov

Blue Hill regulates bars and restaurants through a combination of its 2024 Commercial Site Plan Review Ordinance (CSPRO) and a 1978 Special Amusement Ordinance. These rules give the town authority to manage high-impact development and place conditions on venues that serve alcohol and host live entertainment.

Live Entertainment & Hours

REF: SPECIAL AMUSEMENT ORDINANCE SECTIONS 201 & 204

Any on-premises liquor licensee hosting music, dancing, or entertainment must obtain a Special Amusement Permit. The Select Board can set written rules specifying permitted hours of operation, facility size, and location to protect public welfare.

Traffic and Safety Access

REF: CSPRO SECTION 6.B

The layout must provide safe vehicular and pedestrian movement. Clear access routes must be maintained for emergency vehicles, and parking designs must prevent vehicles from backing out onto a street.

Lot Line Lighting

REF: CSPRO SECTION 6.E.2

Lighting must be shielded so elements are not visible to motorists or abutters. Illumination cannot exceed 0.5 foot-candles upon residences on abutting lots, preventing patio and parking lights from becoming a nuisance.

Emissions and Odors

REF: CSPRO SECTION 6.E.1

The ordinance prohibits emissions that pose an unreasonable risk and explicitly forbids land uses from producing unreasonable, offensive, or harmful odors perceptible beyond the property lines.

Blue Hill Ordinance Excerpts

Special Amusement Ordinance — Section 201 (Permit Required) & Section 204 (Rules and Regulations)

Sec. 201: No licensee for the sale of liquor to be consumed on his licensed premises shall permit, on his licensed premises, any music, except radio or other mechanical device, any dancing or entertainment of any sort unless the licensee shall have first obtained from the municipality... a special amusement permit...
Sec. 204: The municipal officers are hereby authorized... to establish written rules and regulations governing the issuance, suspension, and revocation of special amusement permits... These rules and regulations may specifically determine the location and size of permitted premises, the facilities that may be required... and the hours during which the permitted activities are permitted.

CSPRO — Section 6.B (Traffic), Section 6.E.2 (Lighting) & Section 6.E.1 (Emissions)

Sec. 6.B: The layout of the site shall provide for the safe movement of passenger, service, and emergency vehicles... Clear routes of access shall be provided and maintained for emergency vehicles to all portions of the site... The layout and design of access ways and parking areas shall prevent their backing out onto a street.
Sec. 6.E.2: No land use activity shall be permitted to produce unreasonable glare or brightness beyond the lot lines. Lighting fixtures shall be shielded or hooded so that the lighting elements are not exposed to normal view of motorists, pedestrians, or buildings on abutting lots. Direct or indirect illumination shall not exceed 0.5 foot-candles upon residences on abutting lots.
Sec. 6.E.1: Emissions of dust, dirt, fly ash, fumes, vapors or gases which pose an unreasonable risk of harm to human health or the environment shall be prohibited. No land use activity shall be permitted to produce unreasonable, offensive, or harmful odors perceptible beyond the lot lines, measured either at ground or habitable elevations.

Brooklin

Official Website: brooklinmaine.com

The Brooklin Site Plan Review Ordinance strictly regulates bars and restaurants as "new nonresidential uses" through commercial performance standards. The Planning Board holds the authority to attach specific operational conditions to a permit to ensure a proposed venue meets these baseline criteria.

Noise Limits

REF: SITE PLAN SECTION 9.23

Uses generating noise must be designed and landscaped to minimize interference with neighbors. The ordinance explicitly caps noise levels by enforcing the commercial standards set in Appendix B of Brooklin's Wind Energy Facility Ordinance.

Traffic and Safety Access

REF: SITE PLAN SECTIONS 9.2 & 9.3

The applicant must obtain a letter from the Fire Chief confirming adequate emergency access. Access points must meet DOT sight distances and cannot be placed within 50 feet of an intersection. The Institute of Traffic Engineers manual classifies dining/drinking places as high-trip generators, meaning a busy venue could trigger stricter traffic mitigation or shared access requirements.

Lot Line Lighting

REF: SITE PLAN SECTION 9.22

Exterior lighting must be shielded from neighboring properties and the street. Light spillover cannot exceed 0.5 foot-candles at the property line, strictly limiting bright outdoor patio or parking lot lighting from intruding on residential abutters.

Commercial Setbacks

REF: SITE PLAN SECTION 9.1

The building must be set back at least 25 feet from town roads, 50 feet from state highways, and 15 feet from side and rear property lines. The building height cannot exceed 35 feet.

Brooklin Ordinance Excerpts

Section 9.1 (Utilization of the Site) & Sections 9.2 / 9.3 (Road System & Access)

Sec. 9.1: Buildings must be set back at least twenty five (25) feet from the edge of traveled portions of existing town ways and fifty (50) feet from the edge of traveled portions of a state highway, and must have minimum side and rear lot setbacks of fifteen (15) feet. Buildings shall not exceed thirty-five (35) feet in height.
Sec. 9.2 & 9.3: Vehicular access to the site must be on roads which have adequate capacity... Sec. 9.23: Land uses involving noise in excess of that which is normal for the area are designed and landscaped to minimize noise interference with neighboring uses. Noise emanating from the proposed use shall not exceed the standards set forth in Appendix B "Noise Control" of the Town of Brooklin Wind Energy Facility Ordinance.

Brooksville

Official Website: brooksvillemaine.org

Brooksville manages commercial venues serving alcohol directly through its comprehensive Special Amusement Permit Regulations, which impose strict noise and nuisance controls on any facility hosting live entertainment.

Live Entertainment & Noise

REF: SPECIAL AMUSEMENT REGS SECTIONS 13 & 15

Any on-premises liquor licensee hosting music or entertainment must obtain a Special Amusement Permit. The applicant must explicitly demonstrate the ability to prevent the emanation of excessive noise, and sources of noise must be muffled so as not to be objectionable.

Nuisance Control

REF: SPECIAL AMUSEMENT REGS SECTION 16

The ordinance explicitly forbids licensees from permitting the use of the premises to result in any continued, excessive or loud noise that endangers the comfort, repose, health, or safety of individuals, or which disturbs the peace and tranquility of the neighborhood.

Public Notice & Abutters

REF: SPECIAL AMUSEMENT REGS SECTION 3

Before a permit is granted, the Selectmen must hold a public hearing. All owners of land within 300 feet of the applicant's premises must receive written notice via first class mail.

Castine

Official Website: castine.me.us

Castine subjects commercial developments to rigorous performance standards outlined in its Zoning Ordinance, ensuring that high-impact venues like restaurants and bars do not degrade the historic and residential fabric of the town.

Strict Noise Decibel Limits

REF: ZONING ORDINANCE SECTION 6.21

Castine explicitly caps unreasonable noise at the lot lines. Noise cannot exceed 60 dBA from 7:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m., and is restricted to 50 dBA from 10:00 p.m. to 7:00 a.m.

Parking Ratios

REF: ZONING ORDINANCE SECTION 6.22.B.12

Restaurants and cocktail lounges must provide adequate off-street parking, calculated at one (1) space for every four (4) customer seats, plus one (1) space for every two (2) employees.

Lighting Limitations

REF: ZONING ORDINANCE SECTION 6.14

No land use may produce unreasonable glare beyond its lot lines. Light spillover cannot exceed 0.3 foot-candles. Where practicable, lighting must be directed downward and the light source must be shielded.

Fire Hazard Mitigation

REF: ZONING ORDINANCE SECTION 6.9

Commercial district uses require written approval from the Fire Chief. There must be unobstructed access for fire trucks and ambulances, and an adequate water supply for fire suppression must be available.

Castine Ordinance Excerpts

Zoning Ordinance — Sections 6.9, 6.14, 6.21 & 6.22

Sec. 6.9 (Fire Hazard Mitigation): There shall be no change of use of a structure and no structure shall be built, moved, replaced, altered or enlarged without consultation with and approval in writing by the Fire Chief... Fire trucks and ambulances shall have unobstructed access to the structure... Adequate water supply for fire suppression is or will be available prior to granting a certificate of occupancy.
Sec. 6.14 (Lighting): No land use or establishment shall be permitted to produce unreasonable glare or brightness (0.3 foot-candles) beyond its lot lines. Where practicable, lighting shall be directed downward and the light source shall be shielded. All exterior lighting shall be designed to ensure safe movement of people and vehicles, and to minimize adverse impact on neighboring properties...
Sec. 6.21 (Noise): No land use or establishment shall be permitted to produce unreasonable noise perceptible beyond their lot lines... Unreasonable noise shall be noise exceeding the following levels measured on an ANSI Type 1 or 2 meter using the A-weighted network: 7:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m. - 60 dBA; 10:00 p.m. to 7:00 a.m. - 50 dBA.
Sec. 6.22 (Off-Street Parking B.12): Restaurants and cocktail lounges: one (1) space for each four (4) customer seats, plus one (1) space for each two (2) employees.

Orland

Official Website: orlandme.org

Orland utilizes a comprehensive Site Plan Review Ordinance to evaluate nonresidential construction and uses. The Planning Board strictly evaluates traffic, parking, and neighbor impacts before granting approvals.

Operational & Impact Controls

REF: SITE PLAN ORDINANCE SECTION 9.22

All features that may have an adverse audio or visual impact on surrounding properties must have sufficient setbacks and screening. The town mandates that hours of operation, noise levels, and exterior lighting be designed specifically to minimize adverse impacts on neighboring properties.

Traffic & Safety

REF: SITE PLAN ORDINANCE SECTIONS 9.2 & 9.3

Vehicular access must not cause an intersection to function below a Level of Service D. Driveways must meet MDOT sight distances, and private entrances must be located at least 50 feet from the closest unsignalized intersection.

Parking Design

REF: SITE PLAN ORDINANCE SECTION 9.6

Parking areas with more than two spaces must be arranged so vehicles do not have to back out into the street. All parking spaces and impervious surfaces must be located at least five feet from any side or rear lot line.

Sedgwick

Sedgwick controls commercial venues through both a Site Plan Review Ordinance and a Special Amusement Permit Ordinance, combining land-use design standards with strict operational curfews for entertainment.

REF: SPECIAL AMUSEMENT ORDINANCE SECTION 2.I.3

For premises holding a Special Amusement Permit, all outdoor permitted activities must completely cease at 9:00 PM. All indoor permitted activities must cease at 11:00 PM.

REF: SITE PLAN ORDINANCE SECTION III.B.1

Restaurants, eating, and drinking establishments must provide one (1) off-street parking space for every four (4) seats, plus one (1) space for every two (2) employees.

REF: SITE PLAN ORDINANCE SECTION II.B

Storage areas, machinery, loading areas, and utility buildings must have sufficient setbacks and screening to minimize their adverse impact on surrounding properties.

Surry

Official Website: townofsurrymaine.com

Surry's Unified Development Ordinance (UDO) establishes rigorous site plan review standards for commercial facilities, mandating structural buffers, dark-sky lighting, and stringent parking minimums.

Noise & Structural Buffers

REF: UDO SECTION IV.1

Surry uses mandatory structural buffer strips to minimize noise and visual impacts. No commercial use may be erected unless a buffer strip at least thirty (30) feet wide is provided and maintained between any adjoining residential district or use and the commercial structure. If natural features are insufficient to buffer the noise and view, a landscaped area or a 6-foot fence is required.

Dark Skies Lighting

REF: UDO SECTION IV.14

All exterior lighting must encourage "dark skies" and be designed to minimize adverse impacts on neighboring properties. Adverse impact is judged in terms of hours of use and potential damage to the value of adjacent properties.

Parking Capacity

REF: UDO SECTION IV.18

It shall be unlawful to use any required off-street parking or loading facilities for motor vehicle repair work... No area designated as a required parking area in connection with any designated building or use shall be operated as a commercial or public parking lot... Restaurants, eating & drinking establishments: 1 per 4 seats plus 1 per shift employee.
